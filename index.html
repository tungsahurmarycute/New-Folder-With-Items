What happens when drowning becomes a medical emergency?

Drowning is first a breathing problem. When oxygen delivery is interrupted, the lungs, heart, and brain begin to struggle in sequence. This is a simplified public version of the clinical chain: aspiration, alveolar injury, hypoxemia, cardiac arrest, and post-arrest recovery.

Medical chain

From water exposure to oxygen failure

  • Water enters the airway and alveoli, interrupting normal gas exchange.
  • Fluid and inflammation damage the air sacs and reduce oxygen transfer.
  • Severe hypoxemia can trigger cardiac arrest, then brain injury if oxygen is absent too long.
What goes wrong?

The body stops delivering oxygen effectively.

The problem is not just “water in the lungs.” Drowning causes a cascade of physiologic injury: airway obstruction, surfactant disruption, alveolar collapse, fluid leakage, worsening oxygen transfer, and eventually severe hypoxemia.

1. Airway

Airway and alveoli are hit first.

Water enters the respiratory tract. The air spaces can no longer fill properly, and the normal gas exchange process starts to fail.

2. Inflammation

Fluid and swelling worsen the damage.

The alveolar-capillary barrier becomes leaky. Fluid accumulates, oxygen transfer falls, and the lungs become less able to ventilate normally.

3. Hypoxemia

Oxygen levels drop dangerously.

The blood leaves the lungs poorly oxygenated. With severe hypoxemia, the heart and brain begin to fail.

Key point

Why oxygen matters so much

PaO₂ 50

In severe hypoxemia, the blood is not carrying enough oxygen even with high inspired oxygen. This is why the body can deteriorate rapidly, even before respiratory failure looks obvious at the bedside.

Critical idea

Hypoxia can lead to cardiac arrest.

When the heart and brain are starved of oxygen long enough, the rhythm can fail and circulation stops. Cardiac arrest after drowning is usually the result of the oxygen crisis rather than a separate primary cardiac event.

Timeline

From submersion to recovery

Recovery depends on how long oxygen was interrupted, how severe the lung injury is, and how well the brain and heart are protected after circulation is restored.

Step 1

Submersion

Breathing is impaired and oxygen delivery stops.

Step 2

Aspiration

Water and inflammatory injury reach the airways and alveoli.

Step 3

ARDS-like physiology

Fluid leakage, collapse, and shunt worsen oxygenation.

Step 4

Cardiac arrest / ROSC

Circulation may stop, then return with resuscitation.

Important: a patient can survive the event but still face brain injury, pulmonary edema, and prolonged organ vulnerability after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The recovery stage is not just “the heart is beating again”; it is about protecting the brain and other organs from secondary injury.
Recovery and care

After ROSC, clinicians focus on protection

Oxygenation, blood pressure, ventilation, temperature management, and neuroprognostication all matter. A single early test should not decide the whole story.

Oxygen

Protect oxygen delivery

Oxygenation and ventilation need to be stabilized early and continuously monitored.

Brain

Protect the brain

Hypoxic-ischemic injury can cause swelling, seizures, and neurologic dysfunction.

Timing

Interpret over time

Serial exams, EEG, CT/MRI, and temperature management help guide prognosis and care.

“The goal is not only to restart the heart, but to prevent secondary injury and help the brain recover while the body stabilizes.”
